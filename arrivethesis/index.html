India · The Verified-Identity Network for Homes

The verified identity of India's consumption class — owned by no one. Until now.

Almost all of India's highest-consumption households now live behind a gate — and that share only grows as the country urbanises. Knowing who really lives where, and letting them prove it in one tap, is the single most useful thing for those households — and the single most valuable thing for everyone trying to reach them. Arrive builds that graph, starting at the one place identity is checked every day: the gate.

The Big Idea

A verified-identity graph of India's most valuable households. Convenience makes households join for free; commerce and agents pay to reach them. One asset, two sides, each making the other stronger.

The gate stops asking. Every legitimate arrival authorises itself — from the order you placed or the WhatsApp you already sent — so your phone never buzzes at the gate again. The only person ever stopped is the one nobody invited.

The wedge: start at the gate

The gate is where identity is already checked every single day — the one place to bootstrap the graph with a workflow all three parties (resident, guard, committee) adopt at once. From there the same identity radiates to property, corporate and beyond.

Why platforms won't build it alone

Each has identity + orders, but a society won't run a Blinkit gate app and a Swiggy one and an Uber one. They plug into one neutral layer. Neutrality is the moat — valuable because shared, un-ownable by any single player.

The compounding moat
More verified households → more valuable to commerce & agents → more spend → more convenience → more households. A two-sided graph with a private last-100m map and cross-context identity is far harder to copy than any single-category product.

The risks that decide it — and the honest answer

Platform cold-start

Why would Blinkit or Uber integrate with a sub-scale startup? Answer: we don't need deep integration to begin — start with order-message parsing and dynamic passes, prove riders-per-hour gains in dense clusters, and let household density pull partners to the table. The first anchor partner de-risks the rest.

Platforms build it themselves

Each already holds identity + order + address. Answer: a society won't run a Blinkit gate app and a Swiggy one and an Uber one. They plug into one neutral layer. The society-side infrastructure — guard app, access policy, map, standard — is the half no single platform will rebuild alone. Neutrality is the moat.

Building the verified graph is hard

Verification and last-100m maps are real, expensive work. Answer: that difficulty is the moat. The gate workflow generates both as a by-product of daily use — guard walkthroughs, rider traces, resident corrections — so the asset compounds and can't be shortcut by a fast follower.

RWA / society adoption is slow

Committees are fragmented and political. Answer: partner-funded pilots, developer/greenfield societies at handover (no incumbent to displace), a visible treasury share, and winning the daily-felt experience for guards and residents so switching back to paper is unthinkable.

Privacy & trust (household movement data)

Commercialising who-comes-and-goes would destroy the trust the network depends on. Answer: least-privilege by design — the guard sees only what a decision needs; access data never becomes an ad profile. Trust is the product, protected as DPDP-grade architecture, not an afterthought.

Incumbent response

MyGate can copy the gate feature — it already has. Answer: the feature was never the moat. What no single-category player will build is the neutral graph that spans society, commerce, office and property — becoming shared infrastructure for its own rivals. We compete on the asset and its neutrality, not feature parity.

Why now

Quick-commerce flipped the visitor mix from stranger to platform-verified, making identity checkable at scale for the first time. The categories are fragmented and un-owned. The window to set the neutral standard is open — briefly.
